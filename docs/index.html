Towards Fairness under Label Bias in Image Segmentation

Impact, Measurement and Mitigation

Aditya ParikhStella FrankSneha DasAasa Feragen
Technical University of Denmark (DTU Compute)
arXiv PDF Code BibTeX

When annotation quality or style differs systematically between demographic groups, a segmentation model inherits that label bias. Worse, the usual way to detect it, comparing against clean labels, is exactly what we do not have. This page walks through the paper as a story: what label bias is, why our rulers are bent, how Confident Learning gives us a proxy audit, why biased labels make groups more separable, and how we turn that into a mitigation.

1 What is label bias?

Two annotators, two groups, one dataset

Label noise is random: anyone can make a sloppy mask on a bad day. Label bias is structured: the style or quality of annotations depends on who is in the image. If Fin labels one group and Cole labels the other, the dataset silently encodes "group B objects are bigger" (or smaller) even though the objects are not.

2 Label bias in segmentation

Pixels have geometry, so bias has a direction

Unlike a flipped class label, a biased mask is still mostly right. The error lives at the boundary and points somewhere: deficit (under-segmentation, e.g. erosion) or excess (over-segmentation, e.g. dilation or "bloated" annotations). We build controlled bias with morphological operations on CelebAMask-HQ, and use naturally occurring annotation styles on PhC-U373 cells and IMA++ dermoscopy.

3 The biased ruler

You cannot measure a bent mask with a bent ruler

Fairness audits compare Dice or IoU across groups. But those scores are computed against the observed labels, the very labels that are biased. A model that faithfully reproduces Cole's bloated masks scores perfectly on Cole's test set. The true disparity, which only clean labels reveal, can be several times larger, or smaller, or move in the opposite direction.

Takeaway. Evaluation against observed labels hides label bias instead of detecting it. We need an audit signal that does not require clean labels at all. Enter the detective.
4 Confident Learning as an audit

Let the model's confident predictions be the second opinion

Confident Learning (CL) estimates label errors by comparing observed labels with cross-fitted, confidently predicted labels. We adapt it to segmentation: for each image, confident pixels where observed label and CL prediction disagree are split into excess (label says foreground, model says background) and deficit (label says background, model says foreground). Three numbers summarise the audit per group:

LER

Label Error Rate. (excess + deficit) normalised by the foreground. How much suspected error.

DR

Directional Rate. (excess − deficit) normalised. Which way: positive = over-segmentation, negative = under-segmentation.

RR

Relative Risk. LERgb / LERgc. Where the errors concentrate. RR ≫ 1 flags a group.

What CL gives us

  • A proxy audit without clean labels: prevalence (LER), direction (DR), concentration (RR).
  • Robust to global miscalibration: CL thresholds adapt, so temperature scaling leaves the LER and DR gaps unchanged (Table 9).
  • Validated retrospectively against clean masks: detected errors track injected bias in both magnitude and direction (Table 8).

Where CL stops

  • Needs a competent model. If the segmenter cannot be confident, nothing is flagged.
  • Majority bias is invisible. At β = 100% erosion on CelebA, the model learns the eroded style and CL under-reports it (the ⚠ in the figure). The audit sees disagreement, not truth.
  • Style vs. error is a judgement call. RR says where disagreement concentrates; deciding which group's style is "right" needs domain knowledge. Skin tone from ITA is also only a proxy for demographic group.
5 The separability effect

Biased labels teach the encoder to tell the groups apart

To fit two annotation styles with one decoder, the network must first know which style to produce. So it learns the group. In the encoder's latent space, gc and gb drift apart as β grows, far beyond what visual differences alone cause. Most fairness methods try to erase this signal. We do the opposite: it is exactly the handle a mitigation can grab.

6 Mitigation

Learn every style, then predict in the cleanest one

If the model is going to learn annotation styles anyway, make it explicit. The Subgroup-Conditioned Decoder (SCD) injects a group code into the decoder through FiLM layers, so each group's style gets its own set of feature modulations. At inference we condition every image on the reference group gc, and the biased style never leaves the training set. Auto-SCD removes the need to know which group is cleaner: after a few warm-up epochs the group with the lower training loss is selected as reference (clean labels are easier to fit than corrupted ones).

What else we checked

  • Backbones: ResNet-34 and a ViT encoder give the same picture (Table 12).
  • Bias geometries: erosion, dilation, high-boundary distortion and mixed annotator-level bias (Table 15).
  • Auto-SCD selects the right reference in every biased configuration tried, and is harmless when there is no bias (Table 16).
  • Under a training budget, SCD matches a style-separating baseline that needs the reference group given (Table 14).
7 Takeaways

The story in four lines

1. The ruler is bent

Dice and IoU against observed labels hide label bias and cannot see when a mitigation works.

2. Audit with confident disagreement

CL gives LER, DR and RR from training data alone: how much, which way, and in which group.

3. Bias makes groups separable

The encoder learns the group to fit the style. Suppressing that signal does not fix the labels.

4. Condition, then choose the clean style

SCD models the styles explicitly; Auto-SCD finds the reference group from warm-up loss and closes the true gap.

Limitations. Conditioning needs annotation style to correlate with group; when styles overlap, gains shrink. CL needs a reasonably good segmenter and under-reports bias that the model has fully absorbed. Skin tone estimated from ITA is an imperfect proxy, and real-world label bias may not look like our synthetic corruptions.
